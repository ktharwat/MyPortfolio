Pharma · 2022→now · Software Development Lead

DevOps adoption at Eva Pharma.

Moving a cross-functional engineering organization from manual release gates to automated pipelines — while releases kept shipping the whole way.

Context

Eva Pharma is a pharmaceutical manufacturer; the systems my team builds serve business operations that cannot tolerate downtime. I lead nine developers across several concurrent projects, alongside product, QA and infrastructure stakeholders.

Challenge

Reaching production depended on manual gates: slow feedback, quality checks applied inconsistently, and deadlines that were treated as hopes rather than outputs of a process.

Constraints

  • A regulated-industry environment where production is never an experiment
  • Teams mid-sprint who could not pause for a tooling revolution
  • An existing Microsoft-stack investment (.NET, SQL Server) worth building on, not replacing

My role

End-to-end owner of the adoption: pipeline architecture, rollout sequencing, and the review culture that would protect the new road once it existed.

Decisions

Azure DevOps as the single road to production.

The organization was already Microsoft-native. One system unifying boards, repositories and pipelines beats three tools glued together — integration debt avoided by decision, not by luck.

Automate the gates people argued about first.

Code-review policy and pull-request validation builds came before automated deployment. Settle the most painful argument first, and the rest of the change sells itself.

Roll out team by team, not all at once.

Early-adopter squads became living proof for the next ones. Internal references persuaded more engineers than any mandate could.

Execution

The pipeline became the only road to production: a validation build on every pull request, structured code reviews as policy rather than favor, and continuous mentoring until following the process felt like the normal way to work instead of a project with an end date.

Results

+20%
delivery efficiency across cross-functional teams, measured after rollout
100%
of changes flowing through the same reviewed, automated path
9
engineers shipping through the pipeline without bypasses

Business impact

Releases became predictable enough to promise, multiple projects now run concurrently without competing for manual attention, and stakeholders stopped chasing status because the pipeline reports it. That predictability is what made scaling the team possible.

Lessons

Tooling adoption is social engineering that happens to involve software. The pipeline isn't infrastructure — it's a product whose users are engineers, and it succeeds exactly as much as they choose to use it.
